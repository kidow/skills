# Georges Seurat

A photo repainted as a Seurat-style Neo-Impressionist pointillist painting, built entirely from small, separate dots of pure color, with calm, simplified, sculptural figures and still, luminous light.

## Prompt

```text
Convert the attached photo into an original painting in the style of Georges Seurat's pointillism (Neo-Impressionism, 1884–1891). Don't copy any specific existing Seurat painting.

Dots: build the entire image only from very fine, distinct, round or slightly oval dots of oil paint, about 150 to 250 dots across the width of the image, so the picture reads smoothly from a distance. Scatter the dots irregularly like hand-applied paint dabs, slightly varying in size and shape, and never arrange them in rows, a grid, or a regular mosaic or bead pattern. No long brushstrokes, blended areas, outlines, or smooth gradients anywhere.

Color: use mostly pure, unmixed colors placed side by side so they blend optically from a distance. Inside every area, mix dots of at least three or four different hues, such as greens with blue, yellow, and orange dots in grass or a green garment, reds with orange, pink, and violet dots in a red garment, and blues with violet and orange dots in shadows. Up close the colors must look mixed, never a single flat hue. Place complementary colors next to each other to make light shimmer, and use bright, chalky, slightly pastel tones overall.

Light: calm, even daylight with soft, clear shadows. Where a light area meets a dark area, make the light side a little lighter and the dark side a little darker along the edge, creating a subtle halo of contrast.

Form: simplify people and objects into calm, clean, rounded, almost sculptural shapes with smooth silhouettes and fewer folds and details. Make the whole scene feel quiet and still, as if frozen in time, with people shown mostly in profile or simple frontal views.

Subject: keep the photo's subject, setting, composition, poses, and main colors recognizable. Don't add people, objects, or costumes that aren't in the photo.

Surface: oil paint dots on a light canvas, with tiny gaps of pale canvas showing between some dots. No photographic detail, digital filters, or halftone printing patterns.

Show only the painting filling the frame, with no frame, wall, or painted border.

Don't include any text, signature, or watermark.

Keep the same aspect ratio as the attached image.
```

## Observed in references

These traits come from viewing Google image results for "georges seurat paintings" and from the sources below. The images weren't stored. Add the ones you want to the image prompt.

- **Scenes:** most works show leisure by the river or in parks, such as people relaxing on grass, strolling with parasols and top hats, boats, and calm seascapes.
- **Figures:** people are stiff and doll-like, often in profile, with crisp silhouettes and long, quiet shadows.
- **Dot scale:** the dots are fine enough that the image reads clearly from a distance, though close-up details show colorful mixed dots.
- **Borders:** some paintings have a dotted border painted in contrasting colors, which this style leaves out.
- **Drawings:** Seurat also made black-and-white conté crayon drawings on rough paper, which this style doesn't follow.
- **Overlap:** `claude-monet` uses loose, broken brushstrokes and changing light, while this style uses only even dots and still, orderly forms.

## Examples

- [bicycle-repair.webp](examples/bicycle-repair.webp): conversion of a generated photo of two people repairing a bicycle on a street, made with `gpt-image` using the fine-dot, irregular-scatter, mixed-hue, and calm-figure rules now in the prompt, given as additions at the time. Without those rules, an earlier attempt lined up large dots like a uniform mosaic, with each area close to one hue. This result has no text or border, keeps the 3:2 ratio and the source's people, poses, expressions, clothing, bicycle, and street, and builds everything from scattered dots in bright, chalky colors, with green, blue, and yellow mixed in the sweater and lilac and peach in the pavement. Compared with Seurat's paintings, the dots are still larger, and the figures keep their lively poses instead of looking still and simplified.
